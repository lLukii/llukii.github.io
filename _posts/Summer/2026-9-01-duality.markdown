One of the questions I get asked a lot by others is how on earth I've been juggling robotics while also going to MSM every saturday. The truth is, it's been pretty hard to balance the two activities, not only because of their timing, but because of how distinct these fields really are. 

Of course, robotics and classical piano are both *massive* time commitments, so it isn't uncommon for me to run into scheduling conflicts. I've sent god knows how many absence forms to my piano teacher to catch tournaments and build days, and were times when I needed to skip driver practice to prep for a recital. Nonetheless, my biggest underlying problem is how different these fields are. Of course, most people are involved in more than one EC, but many activities translate well with one another because they're fundamentally part of the same discipline. For instance, many things I learned while being software lead for Ultro were indispensable for working with RL and robotic learning this summer. By contrast, you don't need knowledge of harmonic modulations or how to do glissandos to design a championship-level auto and vice versa. For me, this has made it pretty hard to relate with other people outside of, say, chamber music practice and robotics team meetings. 

I guess you could make a similar argument with sports, since I'm not sure how anything you do in track or football directly helps with robotics. But, I believe the reason this duality has been harder for me is because Browning's sports culture is far stronger than its music culture: the school is actively notified about its athletic teams' milestones through newsletters, instagram posts, and teachers spreading the word. On the flip side, the only updates the school gets about music is maybe its chorus and biannual coffeehouses? Even so, most of the performances are pop music and whatnot, which is, well, NOT what I do at MSM :sob: In other words, people at my school are usually clueless about the 30 minute solo recital I have coming up. Perhaps this problem is excaserbated by going to a really small school, so I'm just hoping to meet more people similar to me wherever I go to college. 

In some ways, I also think the relatively weak music culture at Browning was discoraging to me, especially back in freshman and sophomore year. I vividly remember this one time when a senior outright told me that "being a classical pianist doesn't really mean much." As a result, I didn't exactly try the hardest at MSM, and I spent most of my high school life grinding away at mostly STEM activities instead. By contrast, many music people I know have performed overseas, played in masterclasses, and showcased their skills on programs like NPR's *From The Top*. In hindsight, my biggest regret during high school is not dedicating enough time and care to music earlier. This isn't merely because I could've exchanged more robotics or research sessions for practice hours. Instead, it's because I don't think I was proactive enough showcasing my piano playing to a wider audience, making friends at MSM, and exploring more avenues of music outside of performing, such as composing music. (To be honest, STEM also homogenized me in a way, and the reason I started this blog at all is to reflect on who I *really* am as a person.) 

But hey, in the long run, perhaps being the jack of all trades but master of none is a blessing in disguise? Sure, I'm not as cracked in stem as the bubble of USAMO/RSI/PRIMES out there, and I'm not as cracked in music as the average Juilliard Pre-College kid, but I have a modest mix of both areas. When I actually go into the workforce, I'm sure that I'll cultivate enough technical chops to do fine in a good career. At the same time, music has also had a tremendous impact on my life, so I consider myself very vigilant about technology's impact on the arts—if my boss asks me to create an ML model for music generation, I'd quit on the spot even if he offered me a six-figure bonus. Oh well, let's just hope Suno isn't the only path forward for the music industry...
